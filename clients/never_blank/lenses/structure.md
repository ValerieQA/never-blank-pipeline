This is how a Never Blank article is built. The frame is fixed; the middle is
chosen for the material; the obligations are checked at the end, not marched
through in order. None of the names below may appear as a visible heading or
label in the article.

## The frame

### Opening, about 200 words, these four in this order

1. **Dek — one sentence.** What the article claims, with its number. It stands
   on its own line above the text, not inside the first paragraph.
2. **Hook — one checkable thing.** A figure, a named company, or a
   contradiction. Not a summary, not a generalisation. Put the specific thing in
   and hold the mechanism back: past a point, more specificity in an opening
   lowers response rather than raising it, so keep exactly one variable in
   reserve — how it actually works.
3. **Stakes — why this reaches the reader.** Two or three sentences: what they
   lose by not knowing, and the deadline or condition that makes it theirs.
4. **Concession, when the material has one.** See the concession lens: it is
   evaluated every time and written only when a real, attributable objection or
   limitation exists. Never manufacture one to fill the slot.

### Ending, one element

**Kicker/Echo.** Return to the image or fact from the hook with a new meaning.
This is the last editorial line of the article. Nothing follows it: no call to
action, no "what do you think?", no banner, no sign-off. The working link
belongs earlier in the text, as an ordinary inline link.

## The middle

Front-loading is the default: give the reader the finding early. Withholding is
available where the material earns it — a genuine investigation, a result that
only means something after its setup — and the article has to justify it by
being better that way, not by being more suspenseful.

Choose one pattern for the middle before writing, by what the material is. They
are planning tools. They must never be detectable in the output as a template,
and their step names must never appear in the text.

| What the signal is | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Our own figure, measurement or observation | **Finding**: how it was measured → what came out → how it works → what it costs |
| Something broke, failed, or cost money | **Post-mortem**: what broke → why → what it cost → what changed |
| The market claims X and we have grounds to argue | **Argument**: the claim → where it does not add up → how it actually works → the turn |
| It is unclear how the thing works | **Explainer**: how it works → where it breaks → what to do |
| A specific outside case, profile or text | **Teardown**: the case → what is wrong → the fix → what generalises |

When more than one fits, take the one where we have a visible price we paid:
our own measurement, our own failure, our own hours. Where no pattern fits the
material, write what the material is and say so in the plan — a forced pattern
is worse than none.

The turn — the moment the reading flips — is conditional. It belongs where the
evidence produces one, not in every article.

## Standing obligations

Present somewhere in every article; the place is free.

1. **A portable noun of our own.** See the portable-noun lens: evaluated every
   time, never forced.
2. **Something the reader can check or touch.** A source link, a live example,
   or a procedure stated plainly enough to repeat.
3. **A working link inside the text**, as an ordinary inline link in a
   sentence — never a panel, never in the first screen.
4. **A moment of authorial risk.** One place where the text does not behave
   like a template: an admission, a sharp sentence, a reservation, a stop. This
   is editorial judgement and nothing checks it mechanically. It exists because
   readers penalise the *feel* of machine text regardless of its quality, and
   what they recognise is evenness.
5. **Subheads.** They buy reading time cheaply.

## Consequence

Where the evidence carries a number, the consequence is stated with that
number. Where it does not, state a concrete, falsifiable consequence instead —
something a reader could later find to be untrue. Never a vague benefit.

## Never

- "X is not about A, it's about B" and its variants. Readers keep lists of
  these.
- Two calls to action in a row at the end, or any call to action after the
  Kicker.
- Ending on "what do you think?".
- A call to action in the first screen.
- Compound Presence as its own paragraph. The idea lives across the stream of
  publications, not inside one article.
