Every article is checked for a concession, and gets one only when the material
has one.

A concession is the strongest honest objection to our own claim, stated before
the reader raises it. It qualifies only when all of these hold:

- it is real: someone informed could actually make it;
- it is attributable: it comes from the evidence, a named source, or a
  limitation of what we measured — not from imagining a critic;
- it costs us something: it concedes a point that genuinely weakens our claim,
  rather than one we immediately overturn;
- it is specific: it names the figure, the case or the condition it applies to.

Where the material carries no such objection, write none. An invented
concession, a concession to a straw position, or a concession we knock down in
the next sentence is worse than silence.

Where the evidence limits what we may claim rather than contradicting it, say
what the evidence does not establish — plainly, in one line — and continue.

In an article that argues against a market claim, the concession is not
optional: an argument without one reads as an attack.
